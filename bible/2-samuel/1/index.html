2 Samuel 1

1After the death of Saul, David returned from striking down the Amalekites and stayed in Ziklag two days.
2On the third day a man arrived from Saul’s camp with his clothes torn and dust on his head...
4“The men fled from the battle,” he replied. “Many of them fell and died. And Saul and his son Jonathan are dead.”
6“I happened to be on Mount Gilboa,” the young man said, “and there was Saul, leaning on his spear...
10“So I stood over him and killed him, because I knew that after he had fallen he could not survive. And I took the crown that was on his head and the band on his arm and have brought them here to my lord.” 1
11Then David and all the men with him took hold of their clothes and tore them.
12They mourned and wept and fasted until evening for Saul and his son Jonathan, and for the army of the LORD and for the nation of Israel, because they had fallen by the sword.
14David asked him, “Why were you not afraid to lift your hand to destroy the LORD’s anointed?” 2
16For David said to him, “Your blood be on your own head. Your own mouth testified against you when you said, ‘I killed the LORD’s anointed.’”

The Song of the Bow

“A gazelle lies slain on your heights, Israel.
How the mighty have fallen!” 3
“Saul and Jonathan—
in life they were loved and admired,
and in death they were not parted.
They were swifter than eagles,
they were stronger than lions.”
“How the mighty have fallen in battle!
Jonathan lies slain on your heights.”
“I grieve for you, Jonathan my brother;
you were very dear to me.
Your love for me was wonderful,
more wonderful than that of women.” 4
“How the mighty have fallen!
The weapons of war have perished!” 5

Translation Notes

1 The Amalekite’s Story: In the scrolls, there is a discrepancy between this man’s account and the record in 1 Samuel 31 (where Saul fell on his own sword). Most scholars believe the Amalekite was "Lying for Profit." He likely found Saul already dead, took the crown, and invented a story of "Mercy Killing" to gain a reward from David. He fundamentally misunderstood David’s character.

2 Testified against yourself: David’s decision in the scrolls was based on the "Sanctity of the Anointing." Even if the man was lying, he had confessed to regicide. By executing him, David was distancing himself from any claim that he had "Ascended by Violence." He was establishing that the "Anointed Office" was untouchable by human hands.

3 A Gazelle (Hat-Tze-vi): The word in the scrolls also means "Beauty" or "Glory." This lament is one of the oldest pieces of poetry in the Bible. David was not just mourning a friend; he was mourning the "National Glory" of Israel. It shows that David was a "King of the Heart" before he was a "King of the Land."

4 More wonderful than that of women: In the scrolls, this refers to the "Covenantal Intensity" of their friendship. In a world of political marriages and shifting alliances, Jonathan’s love for David was purely based on "Spiritual Recognition" and "Selfless Sacrifice." It was a "Philia" love that transcended all social norms.

5 The Mighty have Fallen: This refrain in the scrolls serves as the "Theme of the Transition." The era of the "Mighty Man" (the giant Saul) was over. The era of the "Man of the Spirit" (David) was beginning. The "Weapons of War" (Saul’s physical power) had failed, making way for a kingdom based on the "Heart of God."

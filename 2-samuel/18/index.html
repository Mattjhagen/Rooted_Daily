2 Samuel 18

5The king commanded Joab, Abishai and Ittai, “Be gentle with the young man Absalom for my sake.” And all the troops heard the king giving orders to each of the commanders about Absalom. 1
6David’s army marched out of the city to fight Israel, and the battle took place in the forest of Ephraim.
8The battle spread out over the whole countryside, and the forest devoured more men that day than the sword. 2
9Now Absalom happened to meet David’s men. He was riding his mule, and as the mule went under the thick branches of a large oak, Absalom’s head got caught in the tree. He was left hanging in midair... 3
14Joab said, “I’m not going to wait like this for you!” So he took three javelins in his hand and plunged them into Absalom’s heart while Absalom was still alive in the oak tree. 4
31Then the Cushite arrived and said, “My lord the king, hear the good news! The LORD has delivered you today from all who rose up against you.”
The king asked the Cushite, “Is the young man Absalom safe?” The Cushite replied, “May the enemies of my lord the king... be like that young man.”
33The king was shaken. He went up to the room over the gateway and wept. As he went, he said: “O my son Absalom! My son, my son Absalom! If only I had died instead of you—O Absalom, my son, my son!” 5

Translation Notes

1 Be Gentle for my Sake: In the scrolls, this is the "Heart of a Father" conflicting with the "Duty of a King." David knew that Absalom was a traitor, but he still saw him as his "Young Man." This public order placed Joab in a "Legal Dilemma"—follow the King’s sentiment or protect the King’s throne? It shows that David had still not fully learned the lesson of the "Sword" in his house.

2 The Forest Devoured: This is a fascinating military detail in the scrolls. The "Forest of Ephraim" was filled with ravines, pits, and dense thickets. The terrain did more to scatter and destroy Absalom’s disorganized rebels than the actual combat. It implies that "Creation itself" was siding with the Anointed King.

3 Caught in the Tree: In the scrolls, Absalom’s "Glory" (his hair, Chapter 14) became his "Gallows." He was suspended between heaven and earth, belonging to neither. The mule (his royal mount) walked out from under him, leaving him as a "Fruit of Judgment" hanging from the oak. It is a vivid image of "Pride" literally being snagged by the consequences of its own weight.

4 Joab’s Javelins: Joab’s decision in the scrolls was purely "Pragmatic." He knew that if Absalom lived, the rebellion would never truly end. He chose to "Murder the Son" to "Save the Kingdom." His ruthlessness here is the final proof of the "Sons of Zeruiah" being "Too Strong" for David’s heart (Chapter 3). He silenced the king’s rival but broke the king’s heart.

5 If only I had Died: This lament in the scrolls is one of the most agonizing in literature. David was not just mourning a son; he was mourning the "Echo of his own Sin." He saw in Absalom’s death the "Final Result" of his own adultery and murder. His cry to die in Absalom’s place is the ultimate expression of a "Father’s Love" that is willing to take the judgment of the "Wayward Son." This is the highest "Shadow" of the work of Christ in the Old Testament.

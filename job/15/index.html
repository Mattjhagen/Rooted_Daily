Job 15

Undermining Piety

“You even undermine piety and hinder devotion to God.” 1
“Your own mouth condemns you, not mine; your own lips testify against you.”

The Monopoly on Wisdom

“Are you the first man ever born? Were you brought forth before the hills?” 2
“Do you listen in on God’s council? Do you have a monopoly on wisdom?”

The Writhing of the Wicked

“The wicked person writhes in pain all their days... Terrifying sounds fill their ears.” 3
“They will not escape the darkness; a flame will wither their shoots, and the breath of God’s mouth will carry them away.” 4
“They will be like a vine stripped of its unripe grapes, like an olive tree shedding its blossoms.” 5

Translation Notes

1 Undermine Piety (Thaphur Yir’ah): Literally in the scrolls, "You break/frustrate the fear [of God]." Eliphaz is "Uprooting" Job’s spiritual standing. He claims that Job’s "Questions" are a "Danger" to the religious order. He is prioritizing "Orthodoxy" over "Honesty."

2 First Man ever Born (Rishon Adam): The scrolls highlight Eliphaz’s "Sarcastic Theology." He is accusing Job of acting as if he was present at "Creation." He is "Rooting" his own authority in "Tradition" while "Uprooting" Job’s "Personal Experience."

3 Writhes in Pain (Mith-cholel): Literally in the scrolls, "Twisting" or "Dancing in agony." Eliphaz describes the "Psychological Torture" of the wicked. He suggests that even if they are wealthy, they have "Terrifying Sounds" in their ears. He is trying to map "Job’s Current State" onto the "Definition of a Wicked Man."

4 Flame will Wither their Shoots (Yallaheth Yonaq-tho): This is a "Botanical Judgment" in the scrolls. Eliphaz argues that the "Wicked" will have their "New Growth" destroyed by the "Heat" of God’s anger. It is a "Direct Attack" on Job’s "Hope of the Tree" (*Chapter 14*).

5 Like an Olive Tree shedding its Blossoms: The scrolls conclude with this "Image of Futility." Just as an olive tree that sheds its blossoms produces "No Oil," so the life of the wicked produces "No Fruit." Eliphaz is "Rooting" Job’s tragedy in "Inevitability." It marks the end of the "Gentle Phase" of the dialogue. The friends are now "Waging War" against Job’s innocence.
